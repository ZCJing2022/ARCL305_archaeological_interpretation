Throughout this course, we will gather at UBC Vancouver’s Point Grey Campus, located on the traditional, ancestral, and unceded territory of the xʷməθkʷəy̓əm (Musqueam) people. This land has been a place of learning for the Musqueam for millennia.

Course Description

This course introduces students to archaeological interpretation, with a focus on how archaeologists construct, evaluate, and revise explanations of the human past. We begin by examining the nature of archaeological evidence, the role of theory, archaeological classification, theoretical pluralism, and the logic of explanation and argument. We then explore how these interpretive approaches are applied to archaeological problems involving material culture and materiality, technology and skilled practice, mobility and migration, social networks, food and feasting, urbanism, Indigenous and collaborative archaeologies, and cognition and mind. Throughout the course, emphasis is placed on the relationship between theory and archaeological evidence: how concepts shape the questions archaeologists ask, how different forms of evidence support or constrain interpretation, and how competing explanations can be evaluated.

Course Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

  1. Explain key concepts, assumptions, and debates that shape archaeological

interpretation.

  1. Distinguish among archaeological observations, data, evidence, inference,

interpretation, and explanation.

  1. Evaluate archaeological arguments by examining their theoretical assumptions,

evidence, reasoning, and alternative explanations.

  1. Apply theoretical concepts and analytical approaches to archaeological case

studies and datasets.

  1. Compare different theoretical approaches and assess how they produce different

interpretations of the same or similar archaeological evidence.

  1. Construct clear, evidence-based archaeological arguments and communicate

them effectively in discussion and writing.

Prerequisite

ARCL 203 (Archaeological Methods) or ARCL 103 (Introduction to Archaeology: Past Perspectives and Future Promise), or equivalent preparation in archaeology. Students who are uncertain about their preparation are encouraged to consult the instructor.

Required and Optional Readings

All required readings will be made available on Canvas. No specific textbook is required. Two readings are normally required for each instructional week. Additional readings listed in the schedule are optional and are provided for students who wish to explore a topic further, prepare for discussion, or develop a research essay.

Course Format

Class time will be divided approximately equally between lectures and group discussion sessions. Lectures will typically be held on Wednesdays to provide background and context for major themes, while group discussions will take place most Mondays. The schedule varies during weeks affected by holidays or the midterm break. For discussion sessions, the class will be randomly divided each week into small groups of five to six students. Students must complete the required readings in advance and be prepared to contribute substantive comments and critiques. Discussion contributions should engage directly with assigned readings and address specific arguments, evidence, concepts, or questions. Across the course, discussions will repeatedly return to four questions:

  1. What is the author’s main claim?

  2. What evidence supports it?

  3. What theoretical assumptions connect the evidence to the interpretation?

  4. What alternative interpretation might also account for the evidence?

Attendance and participation together account for 24% of the final grade:

  • Attendance and preparation: 8%

  • Discussion participation: 16%

This component evaluates regular attendance, preparation for discussion, substantive engagement with the readings, constructive participation in small-group discussion, and respectful engagement with other students’ interpretations. Students who miss more than one-third of class meetings without an approved academic concession or accommodation will receive no credit for the attendance and participation component.

Assignments

Weekly Reflections — 24%

Weekly reflections are designed to prepare students for discussion and to develop the habit of evaluating interpretations rather than accepting them at face value. Twelve reflections are assigned during the term: one for each topic in Weeks 2–9 and Weeks 11–14. Each reflection is worth 2% of the final grade. Students may complete each reflection through one of two equivalent pathways.

Pathway A: GenAI Critical Evaluation

Complete the required readings before consulting a generative AI system. The purpose of using AI is not to replace reading or generate a summary, but to help you question, test, and deepen your own interpretation of the scholarship. Follow this sequence: Read → Question → Consult AI → Verify → Reflect → Discuss Develop a focused question or interpretive problem from the readings, use a GenAI tool to explore it, and then compare the response carefully with the original texts. Your one-page, single-spaced reflection should focus primarily on your own analysis: what the AI response clarified, overlooked, distorted, or caused you to reconsider. At the end of the reflection, include the principal prompt or question you used and a brief statement explaining how GenAI contributed to your thinking. Full chat transcripts are not normally required.

Pathway B: Scholarly Critical Comparison

Students who prefer not to use a commercial GenAI system may complete an equivalent critical-comparison exercise. Instead of consulting an AI tool, compare two relevant scholarly interpretations or explanations drawn from the assigned, optional, or other appropriate scholarly readings, evaluate them against the assigned readings and archaeological evidence, and use that comparison as the basis for your reflection. Follow this sequence: Read → Question → Compare → Verify → Reflect → Discuss The reflection should identify the interpretive problem, explain where the two interpretations agree or differ, evaluate the evidence and assumptions supporting each, and state your own conclusion.

Submission and Evaluation

Reflections must be submitted online by the following Monday and will serve as the foundation for group discussion. Unless otherwise directed, in-class discussions are AI-free so that students can practice explaining, defending, and revising interpretations through direct engagement with one another. Reflections will be evaluated on engagement with the assigned readings, quality of the interpretive question, critical evaluation of the interpretation being examined, verification against original sources and archaeological evidence, and clarity of the student’s own analysis.

Research Essay — 28%

Students will complete a theory-driven research essay. The process involves:

  1. selecting a subject, theory, or case study covered in the course;

  2. developing a focused archaeological research question;

  3. conducting library research and reviewing scholarly sources beyond the

assigned readings; and

  1. writing an essay that examines how theoretical perspectives, models, and

concepts are used to interpret archaeological data, address research questions, and evaluate arguments. A proposed topic and preliminary research question must be submitted to the instructor for approval by 21 October 2026. A research essay outline, including a preliminary reference list of at least five scholarly sources (books, journal articles, or book chapters), normally including scholarship beyond the assigned course readings, is due 1 November 2026. The final essay will normally draw on a substantially larger body of relevant scholarship. No essay will be accepted unless the topic has been approved in advance. The final essay should be 10–12 double-spaced pages in 12-point font, excluding the title page and bibliography, and must follow the citation style of American Antiquity. Essays will be graded on the relevance and accuracy of theoretical engagement, interpretation of archaeological evidence, quality of argument and reasoning, clarity and organization, and proper referencing. The final essay is due 6 December 2026.

Generative AI and the Research Essay

Generative AI may be used as a support tool for activities such as brainstorming topics, refining research questions, exploring counterarguments, clarifying concepts, identifying organizational problems, or receiving feedback on writing you have already produced. GenAI may not substitute for library research, close reading, source evaluation, archaeological reasoning, or authorship. Do not rely on AI-generated summaries instead of reading sources, submit substantial AI-generated text as your own, or use AI-generated claims or references without independently verifying them. The argument, interpretation of evidence, and substantive analysis must be your own. If GenAI materially contributed to your work, include a brief AI Use Statement at the end of the essay identifying the tool and explaining how you used it and how you evaluated or revised its suggestions. Generative AI is not a scholarly source and should not be cited as evidence for an archaeological claim.

Final Examination — 24%

A take-home final examination will be distributed on 7 December 2026 and must be submitted no later than 13 December 2026. The examination will consist of six to eight essay questions. The examination instructions will specify how many questions must be answered and any length expectations. Students may use generative AI as a study tool before the examination is released. Once the examination has been released, however, generative AI may not be used to generate, develop, organize, rewrite, edit, or otherwise assist with examination answers unless the examination instructions explicitly state otherwise. The examination is intended to assess your own understanding of archaeological theory and your ability to synthesize course material and construct arguments independently.

Assignment Submission and Assessment

All assignments (weekly reflections, proposed research topic and questions, essay outline, research essay, and final examination) must be submitted as Word documents through Canvas unless otherwise directed. Grading will be based on attendance and preparation (8%), discussion participation (16%), weekly reflections (24%), a research essay (28%), and a take-home final examination (24%).

Policy on the Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI), including large language models and other systems that generate text, images, audio, or other content, is now part of the intellectual environment in which archaeological research and learning take place. This course therefore aims to teach students not simply whether AI can be used, but when it contributes to learning and when it interferes with it. The guiding principle for this course is augmentation rather than automation. GenAI can be useful when it helps you ask better questions, explore alternative interpretations, test an argument, clarify a concept, or identify weaknesses in your reasoning. It becomes counterproductive when it performs the intellectual work that you are expected to learn to do yourself. Permitted use varies by assessment and is stated in the relevant assignment instructions:

  • Weekly reflections: GenAI may be used through the structured critical-evaluation pathway; an equivalent non-GenAI scholarly critical-comparison

pathway is also available.

  • Research essay: GenAI may be used as a support tool within the limits stated

above.

  • Final examination: GenAI may not be used after the examination is released

unless the examination instructions explicitly permit a specific use.

  • In-class discussions and designated activities: normally AI-free unless otherwise

specified. Students remain responsible for the accuracy, integrity, and authorship of everything they submit.

Accuracy, Responsibility, and Disclosure

Generative AI can produce convincing but inaccurate information, invented references, distorted summaries, and biased or oversimplified interpretations. Important claims must therefore be checked against original scholarly sources. GenAI output should not be treated as archaeological evidence or scholarly authority. If AI-generated output itself is quoted, reproduced, or analyzed as an object of study, its source should be clearly identified in accordance with UBC guidance. Otherwise, permitted GenAI use should be disclosed/acknowledged through the required AI Use Statement rather than treated as scholarly citation. When GenAI materially contributes to submitted work in a context where its use is permitted, briefly disclose the tool used, what you used it for, and how you evaluated or revised its suggestions. Students should retain relevant prompts, drafts, and records of their process in case clarification is needed. Undisclosed or unauthorized GenAI use that substitutes for work you are expected to perform yourself may constitute academic misconduct.

Privacy, Access, and Instructor Use

Do not upload confidential information, restricted archaeological data, culturally sensitive materials, unpublished work belonging to others, student work, or other information you are not authorized to share with a commercial GenAI system. Exercise similar caution when uploading copyrighted books, articles, lecture slides, or other course materials. No student is required to purchase a paid AI subscription for this course. The non-GenAI critical-comparison pathway for weekly reflections is available to students who do not wish to use a commercial GenAI platform or who have access, privacy, accessibility, or other concerns. UBC distinguishes between GenAI use for personal learning and use in assessed coursework. For assessed work, students must follow the explicit rules established for this course and for each assignment. See UBC Learning with GenAI for current guidance.

Instructor Use of Generative AI

The instructor may use GenAI as a support tool in preparing teaching materials or routine course administration, while remaining responsible for the accuracy, appropriateness, and academic judgment of all course content. Student work will not be submitted to external GenAI systems for assessment or analysis unless the system and use have been approved under applicable UBC privacy requirements and any other required conditions have been met. Final grading and substantive evaluation of student work remain the responsibility of the instructor. AI-detection software will not, by itself, be treated as sufficient evidence of academic misconduct. Questions about authorship or AI use may instead be addressed by discussing a student’s sources, reasoning, drafts, prompts where relevant, and research process. If you are uncertain whether a particular use of GenAI is permitted, ask the instructor before submitting the work.

Academic Concession and Accommodations

Requests for extensions or other academic concessions will be considered in accordance with UBC and Faculty of Arts academic-concession policies. Students should contact the instructor as early as reasonably possible when circumstances affect their ability to complete coursework by a stated deadline. If you miss marked coursework and are an Arts student, review the Faculty of Arts’ academic concession page and then complete Arts Academic Advising’s online academic concession form, so that an advisor can evaluate your concession case. If you are a student in a different Faculty, please consult your Faculty’s webpage on academic concession, and then contact the instructor where appropriate. Academic accommodations reduce barriers in the learning environment for students with disabilities or ongoing medical conditions. Students requiring academic accommodations must register with the Centre for Accessibility, which determines students’ eligibility in accordance with Policy LR7: Disability Accommodation Policy. Instructors are responsible for implementing approved accommodations in consultation with the Centre where necessary. Students are not required to disclose the nature of a disability or ongoing medical condition to the instructor, and instructors should not request disability documentation. Students requiring accommodations should register with the Centre for Accessibility and provide their accommodation letter as early as possible.

Academic Integrity

Academic integrity is essential to the work of this course and to participation in the UBC academic community. Students are responsible for submitting work that represents their own learning and for appropriately acknowledging sources and permitted assistance. Unauthorized collaboration, plagiarism, cheating, falsification, or unauthorized use of generative AI may constitute academic misconduct. If you receive assistance from a tutor or another person, you remain responsible for ensuring that the assistance stays within the limits permitted for the assignment. If you are uncertain about what is permitted for an assignment, consult the instructor before submitting your work. Please consult the UBC Academic Calendar’s Academic Misconduct regulations for the University’s expectations and procedures.

Course Feedback

Feedback and suggestions about the course are welcome throughout the term, either during office hours, after class, or by email.

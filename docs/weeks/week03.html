Week 3 — Levels of Archaeological Interpretation

Central Question

How can archaeologists move responsibly from material traces in the present to explanations of past human behavior, social organization, and historical change?

Archaeological evidence does not narrate the past by itself. Archaeologists observe artifacts, features, sediments, bones, chemical signatures, spatial distributions, buildings, and landscapes in the present. They then construct arguments that connect those observations to claims about activities, institutions, meanings, and processes that usually cannot be observed directly.

This makes Week 3 fundamentally about epistemology: how archaeological knowledge is possible, how it is justified, and how we decide whether one interpretation is better supported than another.

The central position developed in the lecture is:

Archaeological knowledge is theory-mediated but materially constrained.

“Theory-mediated” means that observations and interpretations depend on concepts, classifications, methods, comparisons, background knowledge, and research questions. “Materially constrained” means that archaeologists cannot simply make the past mean anything they want. Evidence can conflict with expectations, weaken an interpretation, support alternatives, and force researchers to revise what they thought they knew.

1. From Evidence to Interpretation

PRESENT OBSERVATIONS
artifacts | features | bones | sediments | isotopes | spatial patterns
                              ↓
                    INFERENTIAL REASONING
              concepts | methods | comparison |
             formation theory | background knowledge
                              ↓
CLAIMS ABOUT THE PAST
behavior | mobility | economy | social organization | historical change

The middle part is crucial. An object does not automatically tell us what happened. We need an inferential warrant: a reason for believing that observed evidence supports a claim.

Finding many imported obsidian blades at a settlement, for example, establishes an observation. It does not by itself establish a market economy. Archaeologists would need to ask how the obsidian arrived, who had access to it, how it was distributed, whether exchange was centralized or decentralized, and what alternative mechanisms could produce the same pattern.

The archaeological record is therefore neither a transparent set of facts nor an arbitrary text.

2. What Is Epistemology?

Epistemology is the study of knowledge: its sources, limits, justification, and reliability.

For archaeology, the problem is unusually sharp because archaeologists normally cannot observe the people and processes they study directly. Banning emphasizes that archaeology shares this difficulty with other historical disciplines that reconstruct past events from evidence available now.

The fundamental question is:

How do we know what we think we know about the past?

How do we know that a stone edge was used for cutting? That a person migrated? That households participated in markets? That political authority became more centralized? Epistemology asks what makes those claims warranted rather than merely possible.

3. Trigger’s Three Epistemological Orientations

Bruce Trigger uses three broad traditions to organize debates about archaeological knowledge:

Orientation Main emphasis Major contribution Major risk
Positivism Observation, hypothesis testing, empirical regularity Explicit and testable arguments Reductionism or overly narrow accounts of human action
Idealism Meaning, intention, culturally mediated perception Recognizes agency, meaning, and theory-ladenness Interpretation can become difficult to constrain if taken too far
Realism External reality plus fallible knowledge Combines interpretation with evidential constraint Requires careful explanation of how evidence constrains claims

These are analytical tendencies, not rigid boxes.

4. Positivism: Observation and Testing

Positivism emphasizes disciplined observation, explicit propositions, comparison, and empirical testing. This orientation strongly influenced processual archaeology or the New Archaeology of the 1960s and 1970s. Processual archaeologists argued that archaeology should move beyond description and chronology toward explanation.

Its strength is methodological explicitness. Researchers should identify what they are explaining, what variables matter, what relationship is proposed, what evidence should exist if the explanation is correct, and what would count against it.

But strict positivist approaches can become reductive. Human beings do not simply respond mechanically to environmental or economic pressures. Meaning, ideology, history, agency, identity, and power can also affect behavior.

Explanation matters, but explanation must not flatten the complexity of human action.

5. Idealism: Meaning and Intention

Idealist approaches emphasize that human action is meaningful. People act within culturally constituted worlds. A building is not simply shelter. A burial is not simply disposal of a corpse. A pot may be simultaneously a tool, commodity, heirloom, status marker, ritual object, or sign of belonging.

R. G. Collingwood emphasized reconstructing reasoning and intentions involved in past action. Later interpretive and postprocessual archaeologies likewise emphasized meaning, symbolism, agency, ideology, and historically situated understanding.

A major insight is the theory-ladenness of observation. Calling something a “house,” “temple,” “elite burial,” “ethnic style,” or even an “artifact type” already involves concepts and classification.

Interpretation is unavoidable; unconstrained interpretation is not enough.

6. Realism: Theory-Mediated but Constrained

Realism provides a way beyond a simple opposition between objective facts and subjective interpretation.

A realist position can hold two propositions simultaneously:

  1. Ontological realism: past events and material processes occurred independently of what archaeologists now think about them.
  2. Epistemic fallibilism: our knowledge is constructed through imperfect concepts, methods, instruments, classifications, and research traditions.

This is why archaeological knowledge can be both constructed and constrained. Trigger argues that archaeological data were created independently of archaeologists and can therefore resist our interpretations.

The point is not certainty. The point is accountability to evidence.

7. Archaeology Between Science, Social Science, and Humanities

Is archaeology a science? The readings show why a simple yes/no answer is not very useful.

Orser describes archaeology as occupying a position between science and history, drawing on systematic empirical methods and humanistic interpretation. Smith argues more specifically that much archaeology belongs comfortably within the modern social sciences.

Smith’s science emphasizes reason and observation, critical testing, provisional findings, interconnected evidence and theory, and evaluation by communities of researchers. It does not require universal laws or laboratory experiments.

This matters because archaeology studies ancient cities, political systems, migrations, inequalities, and long-term historical change—phenomena that cannot simply be recreated in a laboratory.

8. Common Sense Is Not Enough

Urban and Schortman distinguish common sense from systematic theoretical reasoning.

Common sense is indispensable in everyday life, but it is built from culturally learned categories and assumptions that often remain implicit.

Suppose an archaeologist assumes weapons are male, domestic tools are female, large houses are elite, unusual objects are religious, or monumental buildings require centralized states. These propositions may feel intuitive because they fit a familiar worldview. Familiarity is not evidence.

Theory is useful partly because it makes concepts and relationships explicit enough to criticize and test.

9. Explanation

Urban and Schortman treat explanation as an attempt to identify causes and consequences and answer why something occurred as observed.

Description: imported obsidian becomes more frequent in later settlement phases.

Explanation: expanding exchange networks reduced acquisition costs and increased household access to imported obsidian.

The second statement adds a causal mechanism and therefore requires additional evidence.

10. Inference to the Best Explanation

Urban and Schortman emphasize inference to the best explanation (IBE). Several explanations may be compatible with the same observations. Archaeologists compare alternatives and ask which account best explains the available evidence.

Important criteria include:

  • generality — explains a wider and more diverse body of evidence;
  • relative simplicity — avoids unnecessary assumptions;
  • refutability — could be shown to be wrong;
  • expandability — can accommodate relevant new evidence.

“Best” does not mean proven forever. IBE is open-ended.

Do not ask only whether an interpretation fits the evidence. Ask whether alternatives fit it as well or better.

11. Deduction, Induction, and Abduction

Banning distinguishes three forms of reasoning.

Deduction: the conclusion must be true if the premises are true. Stratigraphic reasoning can sometimes take this form.

Induction: evidence supports a conclusion without logically guaranteeing it. Archaeology relies heavily on induction because it works with samples, analogies, probabilities, and incomplete evidence.

Abduction: an informed conjecture or preliminary explanation. An unusual artifact might suggest a possible function, which can then be investigated through residues, use-wear, comparison, or experiment.

Abduction is especially important for generating new questions. It should not be confused with confirmation.

12. Hypotheses, Theories, and Speculation

A hypothesis is a proposition that guides investigation and can be evaluated with evidence. A theory organizes concepts and relationships, generates questions and expectations, and helps explain observations.

Speculation can generate possibilities, but it becomes archaeologically stronger only when an evidential route exists for evaluating it.

question → hypothesis → evidence → evaluation
                ↑                    ↓
                └──── revision ──────┘

Evidence can strengthen, weaken, or redirect hypotheses.

13. Testability: What Would Count Against the Claim?

Smith’s practical question is:

How would you know if you are wrong?

The lecture translates this into:

What evidence would make this interpretation less plausible?

Suppose growing political centralization is proposed to have caused craft production to become more centrally controlled. What else should be visible—production concentration, standardization, controlled raw materials, administrative devices? What observations would weaken the claim?

If every possible result can be redescribed as support, the explanation is difficult to evaluate.

14. Mechanisms: Correlation Is Not Explanation

Settlement size may correlate with craft specialization. Imported goods may correlate with wealth. Population density may correlate with agricultural intensification.

But correlation alone does not tell us why variables are connected.

A causal mechanism specifies a process capable of generating the observed relationship.

OBSERVED PATTERN
       ↓
PROPOSED MECHANISM
       ↓
ADDITIONAL EXPECTATIONS
       ↓
INDEPENDENT TESTS

15. Correspondence, Coherence, and Equifinality

Trigger highlights two tests of explanation.

Correspondence asks whether an explanation fits relevant empirical evidence. A claim that drought caused political collapse requires evidence that drought occurred at the relevant time and place.

Coherence asks whether the argument is logically consistent.

Strong explanations need both.

Equifinality means that different processes can produce similar outcomes. Political decentralization might result from drought, warfare, succession crises, economic disruption, internal political conflict, or combinations of these.

Equifinality is one reason archaeologists must compare alternatives rather than move directly from pattern to cause.

16. Not All Theory Works at the Same Level

The lecture distinguishes three levels:

HIGH-LEVEL THEORY
broad frameworks of society, history, and long-term change
                  ↕
MIDDLE-LEVEL THEORY
testable explanatory propositions about recurrent social processes
                  ↕
LOW-LEVEL GENERALIZATIONS
empirical regularities in archaeological observations

These are not mechanical stages. Archaeologists move back and forth among them.

17. Low-Level Generalizations: Establish the Pattern

Low-level generalizations are closest to archaeological observations.

Examples include pottery types in particular stratigraphic horizons, house forms concentrated in one settlement sector, burial forms changing after a chronological threshold, or obsidian from one source increasing through time.

They answer:

What regularity do we observe?

Trigger emphasizes that correlations among archaeological observations are not yet explanations of human behavior.

Observation:
Later households contain more imported obsidian.

Low-level generalization:
Imported obsidian increased through time.

That is important, but it does not explain why.

18. Middle-Level Theory: Testable Social Explanation

Middle-level theory occupies an intermediate level of abstraction. It asks what recurrent social mechanism might explain an empirical pattern.

Examples might connect:

  • population pressure and agricultural intensification;
  • market expansion and occupational specialization;
  • settlement density and interaction frequency;
  • institutional centralization and administrative infrastructure;
  • inequality and differential access to resources.

Middle-level theories are broader than a single case but empirically specific enough to investigate.

What social process could generate this recurring pattern, and what additional evidence should that process produce?

19. High-Level Theory: Broad Frameworks

High-level theory works at greater abstraction.

Examples in the lecture include cultural ecology, Marxism and historical materialism, structuralism, evolutionary approaches, practice theory, poststructuralism, materiality, and agency.

These frameworks orient attention. They suggest what entities, variables, relationships, or historical processes may matter.

They are not normally confirmed or rejected by one artifact distribution. Their usefulness is assessed indirectly through the coherence, scope, and repeated empirical performance of more specific arguments.

Urban and Schortman’s metaphor is useful: theory resembles a flashlight. It illuminates some aspects of a complex world while leaving others in shadow.

20. Worked Example: Boserup and Agricultural Intensification

Trigger uses Ester Boserup’s proposition as an example of middle-level theory.

Increasing population pressure
              ↓
Greater demand for production
              ↓
Increased labor investment
              ↓
More intensive cultivation

At the low level, archaeologists might document increasing settlement density, terraces or irrigation, changing field systems, agricultural tools, crop changes, or altered land use.

At the middle level, the Boserupian proposition links population conditions to labor investment and agricultural intensification, producing archaeological expectations.

At the high level, results may contribute to broader debates about cultural ecology, political economy, household labor, state formation, or human-environment relations.

Different levels therefore answer different questions.

21. Hawkes’ Ladder of Inference

Christopher Hawkes proposed a famous hierarchy of interpretive difficulty:

technology
    ↓
subsistence and economy
    ↓
social and political organization
    ↓
religious ideas and beliefs

Its lasting value is not that religion is always impossible to infer or technology always easy. The difficulty of an inference depends on context and evidence.

The durable lesson is:

Different claims require different warrants.

22. Formation Processes and the Inferential Bridge

Michael Schiffer’s work directs attention to transformations between human activity and archaeological observation:

use → reuse → discard → deposition → disturbance → decay → sampling → recovery

Before an artifact distribution is used to explain social organization, archaeologists need confidence that the pattern is not primarily a product of formation processes.

This prepares us for Binfordian middle-range theory.

23. The Terminological Problem: Two Meanings of “Middle”

One of Week 3’s most important distinctions is that Robert K. Merton and Lewis Binford used similar terminology for different intellectual purposes.

MERTONIAN / SMITHIAN
MIDDLE-RANGE THEORY
empirical social theory
explains recurrent social mechanisms

             ≠

BINFORDIAN
MIDDLE-RANGE THEORY
linking / formation theory
connects traces to behavior and processes

For teaching clarity, the lecture uses:

  • middle-level empirical theory for the Mertonian/Smithian form of social explanation;
  • Binfordian middle-range theory (MRT) for archaeological linking and formation arguments.

This is a pedagogical and functional distinction. It should not be turned into a false claim that the histories are completely separate.

Trigger explicitly notes that middle-level theory and Binfordian middle-range theory are not identical, because middle-level theory may concern human behavior alone whereas middle-range theory must connect behavior with archaeologically observable traits. But Trigger also treats Binford’s middle-range theory as a special type of middle-level theory.

24. Binfordian Middle-Range Theory: Statics to Dynamics

Binfordian MRT addresses a specific inferential problem:

How can observable material patterns in the present be linked to unobservable behavioral and formation processes in the past?

OBSERVED STATICS
bones | artifacts | hearths | spatial distributions
                    ↓
BINFORDIAN MRT
actualistic comparison | ethnoarchaeology |
experiments | taphonomy | formation processes
                    ↓
INFERRED DYNAMICS
discard | butchery | mobility | activity organization |
occupation history | site formation

The bridge must be argued rather than assumed.

25. Actualistic Research

Actualistic research studies present processes to understand how material patterns are generated.

Important forms include:

Ethnoarchaeology — investigates relationships among observed behavior, material culture, spatial organization, and discard.

Experimental archaeology — reproduces processes such as stone-tool manufacture, burning, building, breakage, or use-wear formation.

Taphonomy — studies processes affecting remains between death, deposition, burial, recovery, and analysis.

These methods do not provide direct analogies that can simply be copied onto the past. They investigate causal relationships between processes and material consequences.

26. Worked Example: Nunamiut Ethnoarchaeology

Binford’s ethnoarchaeological research among the Nunamiut became a classic example of middle-range reasoning.

Paleolithic archaeologists debated why Mousterian stone-tool assemblages differed. François Bordes emphasized recurring assemblage types and interpreted much variation as cultural tradition. Lewis and Sally Binford argued that some variation might instead reflect activities and site function.

Binford’s observations of hearth-centered activities contributed to the distinction between:

  • a drop zone near the activity area, where small debris accumulated;
  • toss zones farther away, where larger unwanted materials were discarded.
             FORWARD TOSS AREA
            larger discarded items

                    ↑

             +----------------+
             |   DROP ZONE    |
             |  small debris  |
             |     HEARTH     |
             | seated activity|
             +----------------+

                    ↓

             BACKWARD TOSS AREA

The importance of the example is methodological, not that it permanently settled every Mousterian debate.

observed behavior
       ↓
behavior–material relationship
       ↓
testable archaeological expectation

27. Mertonian Middle-Range Theory

Robert K. Merton developed theories of the middle range in sociology between two extremes:

  • narrow empirical observations with little explanatory reach;
  • all-encompassing grand theories operating at very high abstraction.

Mertonian middle-range theory identifies social mechanisms general enough to apply beyond one case but specific enough to investigate empirically.

Examples include social mobility, institutional organization, division of labor, markets, reference groups, and social networks.

This performs a different epistemological job from Binfordian MRT.

28. Smith and Empirical Social Theory

Michael E. Smith emphasizes empirically accountable social explanation in archaeology.

This kind of theory has:

  • less abstraction than high-level social theory;
  • more empirical content;
  • explicit concepts and mechanisms;
  • observable implications that can be evaluated archaeologically.

It asks questions such as:

  • How do markets become integrated?
  • How does population concentration alter interaction?
  • How does occupational specialization develop?
  • How do institutions affect access to resources?
  • How does the built environment shape social interaction?

These are questions about how social systems operated, not primarily about how a deposit formed.

29. Worked Example: Markets and Household Production

Imagine archaeologists studying households across several settlements and documenting:

  • imported materials;
  • craft tools;
  • production debris;
  • household goods;
  • settlement distributions.

A middle-level social explanation might propose increasing market integration and occupational specialization.

EMPIRICAL OBSERVATIONS
household goods | imports | craft tools | production evidence
                         ↓
MIDDLE-LEVEL SOCIAL THEORY
market exchange | specialization | division of labor
                         ↓
TESTABLE EXPECTATIONS
broader distributions | production differentiation |
variation in household access | settlement-size relationships

The explanatory question is not simply:

How did these artifacts enter the archaeological record?

It is:

What social and economic mechanisms generated recurrent patterns across households and settlements?

30. The Critical Distinction

Feature Binfordian middle-range theory Mertonian / Smithian empirical theory
Central problem How do material remains relate to past behavior and formation processes? What mechanisms explain recurrent patterns in social organization?
Inferential focus Traces ↔︎ behavior / formation Social patterns ↔︎ causal mechanisms
Typical methods Ethnoarchaeology, experiment, taphonomy, actualistic studies Comparative, institutional, spatial, urban, economic analysis
Example question Why does debris cluster around this hearth? How did market integration affect household production?
Contribution Strengthens links between record and behavior Explains how social systems operate

Neither is inherently “higher” or more important. They solve different epistemological problems.

31. A Better Model of Archaeological Reasoning

A single vertical ladder can be misleading. A better model recognizes two connected inferential tasks:

                    HIGH-LEVEL THEORY
      broad frameworks of society, history, power, ecology
                            ↕
             MIDDLE-LEVEL EMPIRICAL THEORY
      testable mechanisms of markets, institutions,
        labor, inequality, social organization

====================================================

ARCHAEOLOGICAL OBSERVATIONS
artifacts | features | bones | deposits | chemical signatures
                            ↕
             BINFORDIAN MIDDLE-RANGE THEORY
      linking arguments about behavior and formation
                            ↕
               CLAIMS ABOUT PAST ACTIVITIES
      discard | mobility | production | occupation | use

The domains interact. Formation theory may establish that obsidian blades were discarded during household production. Those reconstructed activities can then become evidence in a middle-level explanation of specialization and market exchange.

material traces
      ↓
formation / linking arguments
      ↓
reconstructed activities
      ↓
middle-level social mechanisms
      ↓
broader theoretical interpretation

32. Strong and Weak Theory

Lucas and Witmore challenge another assumption: that theory must always arrive first as a ready-made conceptual framework.

They distinguish strong theory from weak theory.

Strong theory tends to supply a framework in advance. It brings concepts, categories, and expectations to a case and seeks to organize observations through them.

Weak theory deliberately hesitates. It gives greater room for objects, places, anomalies, and unexpected observations to redirect inquiry.

Their example of the “recalcitrant” potsherd is useful. Most sherds fit established categories. One burned sherd does not. Instead of forcing it into an existing box, the archaeologist allows its awkwardness to generate a new question.

Weak theory is therefore associated with slow archaeology, patience, prolonged observation, ambiguity, and openness to unforeseen insights.

This does not mean “no theory.” It asks:

How much should theory determine in advance, and how much should the archaeological situation be allowed to redirect our thinking?

33. Theory After Grand Paradigms

Lucas and Witmore also question the familiar history of archaeology as a succession of dominant “-isms.”

Twentieth-century theory debates were often narrated as competition among culture history, processualism, postprocessualism, Marxism, structuralism, and other paradigms.

Contemporary archaeology is more theoretically plural. Concepts such as agency, materiality, gender, practice, networks, and ontology may be used without total allegiance to one grand school.

But theoretical pluralism does not mean theory disappears.

The danger of declaring “the death of theory” is a return to naive empiricism: the idea that enough data can reveal the past without conceptual mediation.

The Week 3 answer is clear:

There is no theory-free route from archaeological data to historical explanation.

34. Worked Example: Competing Explanations

Imagine a settlement in which later households contain more imported pottery and obsidian than earlier households.

Low-level pattern

Imported goods increase through time.

Possible explanations

  1. regional market exchange intensified;
  2. political elites redistributed imported goods;
  3. migration brought people with different consumption practices;
  4. sampling happened to expose wealthier later households;
  5. chronological mixing inflated the later counts.

Formation/linking questions

Are the deposits contemporary? Are goods in primary contexts? Were later deposits sampled more intensively? Are objects household consumption refuse or redeposited material?

Middle-level social questions

If markets intensified, should access become broader across households? If elite redistribution dominated, should imports cluster near high-status residences? If migration is important, do isotopic or stylistic patterns provide independent evidence?

High-level questions

How would the result bear on wider models of political economy, inequality, urbanization, or regional integration?

The example shows why archaeological interpretation requires several kinds of theory doing different jobs.

35. Guided Practice

Exercise A: Identify the level

Classify each statement as low-level generalization, middle-level social explanation, high-level framework, or Binfordian linking argument.

  1. Red-slipped pottery increases from Phase II to Phase III.
  2. Greater settlement density increased opportunities for specialized exchange.
  3. Experimental burning shows that this surface alteration forms above a particular temperature.
  4. Historical materialism directs attention to relations between labor, surplus, and power.

Then explain why each belongs where you placed it.

Exercise B: Find the mechanism

Observation: larger settlements contain more craft workshops.

Generate two different mechanisms that could produce this relationship. For each, identify one additional archaeological expectation.

Exercise C: Testability

Choose an interpretation from an archaeological article and ask:

  • What observations support it?
  • What would make it less plausible?
  • Is there any possible observation the author would accept as contrary evidence?
  • Are alternatives compared?

Exercise D: Strong versus weak theory

Imagine that one artifact refuses to fit your typology.

A strong-theory response might classify it using the closest existing category.

A weak-theory response might pause and ask why the object resists the classification.

What new research question could the anomaly generate?

Experimental archaeology Controlled investigation of processes capable of producing archaeological traces
Taphonomy Study of transformations affecting organic remains between death and recovery
Strong theory Theory that supplies substantial conceptual structure in advance of engagement with a case
Weak theory Theorizing that withholds ready-made explanation and allows objects and situations to redirect inquiry
Naive empiricism Assumption that data can reveal truth without conceptual or theoretical mediation

39. Key Takeaways

  1. Archaeological evidence is indirect. Archaeologists observe present traces and infer past processes.
  2. Archaeological knowledge is theory-mediated but materially constrained.
  3. Epistemology asks how archaeological claims are justified.
  4. Positivism, idealism, and realism emphasize different dimensions of knowledge.
  5. Theory-ladenness does not make evidence powerless.
  6. Archaeology can operate as a social science without copying an outdated model of natural science.
  7. Common sense contains hidden cultural assumptions; theory makes assumptions more explicit.
  8. Explanation goes beyond description by proposing causes or mechanisms.
  9. Inference to the best explanation compares alternatives rather than merely finding a story that fits.
  10. Most archaeological reasoning is uncertain and inductive.
  11. Abduction helps generate new possibilities; it does not by itself confirm them.
  12. A strong interpretation identifies what evidence would count against it.
  13. Correlation is not explanation; mechanisms connect causes to outcomes.
  14. Correspondence and coherence are both necessary for strong explanation.
  15. Equifinality requires archaeologists to consider alternative causes.
  16. Low-level generalizations establish patterns; middle-level theory explains recurrent mechanisms; high-level theory provides broad frameworks.
  17. Binfordian MRT performs a different job: it links material statics to behavioral and formation dynamics.
  18. Mertonian/Smithian empirical theory explains recurrent social mechanisms.
  19. Actualistic research strengthens linking arguments but does not provide automatic analogies.
  20. Strong and weak theory differ in how much conceptual structure they impose in advance.
  21. Anomalies can be intellectually productive because recalcitrant evidence can redirect inquiry.
  22. Theoretical pluralism does not eliminate theory.
  23. Good archaeological interpretation makes its inferential architecture visible and open to criticism.

40. Bringing the Week Together

The whole lecture can be represented as a sequence:

MATERIAL TRACES IN THE PRESENT
             ↓
FORMATION / LINKING ARGUMENTS
             ↓
RECONSTRUCTED PAST ACTIVITIES
             ↓
EMPIRICAL SOCIAL EXPLANATION
             ↓
BROADER THEORETICAL INTERPRETATION
             ↕
NEW EVIDENCE, ALTERNATIVES, CRITICISM, REVISION

This is not a one-way conveyor belt. Archaeologists move repeatedly among observations, questions, concepts, hypotheses, methods, and explanations.

Theories influence what archaeologists look for, but unexpected evidence can redirect theory. Broad frameworks generate questions, but detailed empirical work can expose their limitations. Linking arguments make traces behaviorally meaningful, while social theory asks why recurrent behavioral patterns occurred.

The goal is therefore not to eliminate theory.

It is to make theory explicit, proportionate to the evidence, open to alternatives, and revisable.

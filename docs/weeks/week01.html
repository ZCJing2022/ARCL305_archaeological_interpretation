Week 1 — Introduction

Week 1 Overview

Archaeologists study people who are no longer available to answer our questions. What survives is usually not a complete account of a past society but a damaged, selective, and transformed material record: artifacts, structures, soils, bones, plant remains, landscapes, images, inscriptions, and many other traces. These traces do not arrive with explanations attached.

That creates the central problem of Week 1:

How can archaeologists build reliable knowledge about human lives from material traces that do not speak for themselves?

The lecture begins with the silent archaeological record and the necessity of theory. It then asks why common sense is not enough, why archaeological observation is theory-laden, and how evidence can nevertheless resist our expectations. The second half carries the same problem into big data, machine learning, and generative AI. More data and more powerful algorithms do not remove interpretation; they introduce new layers of representation, selection, and potential bias.

Questions for This Week

  1. Why can archaeological remains not simply “speak for themselves”?
  2. What distinguishes observation, data, evidence, interpretation, and explanation?
  3. Why is “common sense” not theory-free?
  4. In what sense is archaeological observation theory-laden?
  5. If observations are theory-laden, how can evidence still challenge expectations?
  6. Why can several interpretations sometimes fit the same evidence?
  7. How can multiple independent lines of evidence strengthen an inference?
  8. Why does more data not automatically solve interpretation?
  9. What is lost or transformed when material traces become digital data?
  10. What should remain the responsibility of human archaeologists when AI is used?

Learning Objectives

By the end of Week 1, you should be able to explain why archaeological remains require interpretation; distinguish observation, data, evidence, interpretation, and explanation; identify assumptions connecting evidence to claims; explain theory-ladenness without concluding that “anything goes”; describe underdetermination and unproof; explain why multiple lines of evidence can strengthen inference; identify the proxy gap in computational archaeology; and critically evaluate AI-generated archaeological claims.

1. The Silent Record

Renfrew and Bahn define archaeology broadly as the study of the human past through material remains. They also emphasize that archaeology combines fieldwork, scientific analysis, historical inquiry, interpretation, and creative imagination. The important point for this course is that material things do not tell us directly what to think.

A potsherd does not tell us who made it. A burial does not announce the social identity of the person buried in it. A burned building does not tell us whether it burned accidentally, during warfare, or through intentional abandonment. Archaeologists must build arguments that connect surviving traces to claims about past people.

Term Working meaning Simple example
Observation Something noticed or measured A dark soil layer lies above a floor
Data Observations recorded in usable form Context sheet, coordinates, measurements, photographs
Evidence Data used in relation to a question or claim The layer is relevant to a claim about burning
Interpretation A reasoned account of what evidence means The structure experienced a burning event
Explanation An account of why a pattern or event occurred The structure burned during conflict

The same observation can become evidence for different questions. A tooth might be evidence for diet, mobility, biological relatedness, chronology, or health depending on the research problem.

Key idea: Evidence is relational. Something becomes evidence for a claim.

2. What Theory Does

Theory helps archaeologists decide which questions matter, which observations are relevant, which categories to use, what relationships to investigate, what processes could connect surviving traces to past action, and what would count against an interpretation.

Renfrew and Bahn’s history of archaeology makes the point especially clearly. Archaeology did not become powerful simply because more objects were discovered. The material record had been present all along. What changed were the questions, concepts, theories, and methods through which people learned to investigate it.

Theory is therefore not the opposite of evidence. A better relationship is:

question → concepts/theory → observation and recording → evidence → inference → interpretation → explanation

The sequence is not strictly one-way. Unexpected evidence can change a question. A failed prediction can force a theory to be revised. A new method can reveal a pattern that earlier archaeologists could not see.

3. Common Sense as a Cloak

Why not avoid theory and simply use common sense?

Because what feels obvious often contains assumptions that have not been examined.

Imagine excavating a building containing ash, charcoal, and collapsed roofing. A fire is a reasonable inference. But was it accidental? Warfare? Deliberate destruction at abandonment? A later deposit of burned material? “Common sense” cannot choose among these possibilities without additional assumptions and evidence.

Likewise, precious objects in a grave do not automatically demonstrate that the deceased was a “chief,” “king,” or person of high status. Such an inference may import familiar ideas about wealth, ownership, rank, gender, and individuality into a society organized differently.

Common sense is shaped by our historical period, cultural background, social position, familiar institutions, assumptions about human nature, and disciplinary traditions.

A powerful ARCL 305 question is therefore:

What has to be true for this interpretation to follow from this evidence?

Making assumptions visible makes them available for criticism.

4. The Theory-Laden Trowel

Interpretation does not begin only after excavation. It begins while archaeological data are being created.

An excavator who notices a soil change must decide whether it is significant, where its boundary lies, whether to call it a floor, fill, pit, or destruction layer, which samples to collect, which measurements to take, and how to enter it into a database. These decisions depend on training, research questions, concepts, and expectations.

This is the meaning of theory-laden observation.

Archaeological data are made through disciplined encounters with material remains; they are not simply copied from the ground.

But theory-laden does not mean fabricated. A deposit still has physical properties. Some descriptions fit those properties better than others. Stratigraphy, chronology, chemistry, use-wear, spatial relationships, and other observations can create constraints.

5. The Record Bites Back

Alison Wylie’s work helps us avoid two extremes:

  • naive objectivism: collect enough facts and the past will reveal itself;
  • radical relativism: because interpretation is theory-laden, all interpretations are equally defensible.

Her discussion of archaeological research at Fort Walsh shows researchers beginning with questions and expectations influenced by documentary history and theory. Yet archaeological findings sometimes disrupted those expectations.

The lecture calls this “the record bites back.”

Material evidence can fail to show something an interpretation predicts, reveal an unexpected chronological relationship, contradict documentary expectations, expose variation hidden by a broad category, or make one explanation increasingly difficult to sustain.

Suppose archaeologists hypothesize that a settlement was rapidly abandoned after violent attack. They might expect widespread destruction, a narrow final chronology, objects left in place, and evidence of trauma or weapons. If excavation instead shows repeated rebuilding, cleared rooms, long chronological intervals, and no coherent destruction horizon, the material record does not automatically tell us what happened. But it creates serious problems for the original hypothesis.

Evidence does not need to speak for itself in order to tell us that some interpretations work poorly.

6. Constructive Bootstrapping

How can archaeologists build stronger interpretations when every individual line of evidence is incomplete?

One strategy is to combine multiple, partly independent lines of evidence.

For a question about human mobility, archaeologists might consider strontium and oxygen isotopes, ancient DNA, burial practices, artifact styles, raw-material sourcing, settlement history, and radiocarbon chronology. No single dataset automatically equals “migration.” Each has limitations. But compatible results produced through different methods can mutually constrain interpretation.

Independence matters. Five measurements built on the same classification or chronological assumption are not necessarily five independent lines of evidence.

Ask:

  • Were the datasets produced independently?
  • Do they rely on the same assumptions?
  • Could one source of bias affect all of them?
  • Do different methods constrain different parts of the argument?

Convergence is strongest when different evidential routes reach compatible conclusions without all depending on the same hidden premise.

7. The Bias of Focus

Theory also directs attention.

No archaeological project can record everything. Researchers choose what to excavate, sample, count, photograph, classify, preserve, compare, and ignore. The lecture describes theory as a set of conceptual blinders. Selection is unavoidable; the problem is forgetting that selection occurred.

A project centered on elite political organization may record monuments, inscriptions, and prestige goods in exceptional detail. A project centered on household practice may prioritize domestic deposits, food remains, production debris, and spatial routines.

Neither dataset is simply “the past.” Each is a record produced through a research focus.

When using an archaeological dataset, therefore, ask not only what does it contain? but also why were these things recorded, and what did the recording strategy make difficult to see?

8. The Jigsaw Hypothesis

The jigsaw-puzzle metaphor suggests that the past is a complete picture, discoveries are missing pieces, and enough pieces will eventually reveal the image.

Chapman explains why this is misleading. In a real jigsaw puzzle, the complete picture already exists, the pieces were designed to fit it, all belong to one puzzle, and there is a correct final arrangement. Archaeology has none of these advantages.

The archaeological record has been transformed by deposition, decay, reuse, disturbance, preservation, excavation, and sampling. Many traces are permanently gone. Archaeologists do not know how many “pieces” originally existed, and there is no picture on the box.

More importantly, facts do not automatically assemble themselves into historical meaning.

More data can reduce some uncertainties, but more data do not automatically produce interpretation.

9. Fragmentation, Underdetermination, and Unproof

Catherine Frieman asks archaeologists to take incompleteness seriously rather than treating it only as a temporary technical problem.

Fragmentation

The record is not incomplete merely because archaeologists have not excavated enough. Organic materials decay. Sites erode. objects are recycled. Landscapes change. Some activities leave little durable trace. Excavation and sampling are selective. We often cannot know exactly what has disappeared.

Underdetermination

Underdetermination occurs when the available evidence does not uniquely determine one interpretation.

Two explanations may both fit the surviving evidence. Additional evidence might distinguish them, but sometimes it may not.

This does not mean every imaginable explanation is equally good. Interpretations can still be criticized for contradicting evidence, requiring unsupported assumptions, ignoring contrary observations, depending on weak analogies, or violating chronology.

Unproof

Frieman uses unproof for gaps, ambiguities, unknowns, overlooked possibilities, and things that may remain unknowable.

Her argument is not that archaeologists should stop producing knowledge. It is that smoothing over uncertainty can create unwarranted certainty. Uncertainty can instead generate productive questions:

  • Which assumptions are doing the most work?
  • Whose lives have become archaeologically invisible?
  • Which alternatives have been excluded too quickly?
  • What evidence would distinguish between interpretations?
  • Which questions cannot currently be answered?

A responsible archaeological interpretation can be strong without pretending to be certain.

10. Wylie and Frieman Together

Wylie Frieman
Evidence is interpreted through conceptual frameworks The archaeological record is permanently fragmented
Theory-ladenness does not eliminate empirical constraint Gaps and ambiguity should remain visible
Material evidence can challenge expectations Several possibilities may remain open
Multiple evidential strands can strengthen inference Uncertainty can generate new questions

Together they support a mature position: archaeologists can make well-supported claims while remaining explicit about assumptions, uncertainty, and alternatives.

11. From Observation to Explanation

A useful model is:

material trace → observation → recorded data → evidence for a question → inference → interpretation → explanation

At every arrow, ask what assumptions make the move possible.

Worked example: a burned building

Observation: charcoal, ash, heat-altered clay, collapsed roofing.

Data: stratigraphic descriptions, sample locations, photographs, dates, laboratory results.

Evidence for: a burning episode.

Interpretation: the structure burned near the end of its use-life.

Possible explanations: accidental fire; attack; intentional abandonment; ritual destruction.

The observation of burning does not by itself identify the cause. To distinguish explanations, archaeologists need further expectations and evidence: distribution of burning, human remains, weapons, rebuilding, abandonment patterns, dating, comparable practices, and formation processes.

This is why the course repeatedly asks:

  1. What is the main claim?
  2. What evidence supports it?
  3. What assumptions connect evidence to interpretation?
  4. What alternative explanations could also account for the data?

12. Big Data and the Death of Theory

The lecture now moves into contemporary archaeology.

Large datasets, ancient DNA, isotope analysis, remote sensing, GIS, machine learning, and other computational approaches have transformed archaeological research. These methods can reveal patterns that were previously inaccessible.

But a tempting argument follows:

If we collect enough data and use sufficiently powerful algorithms, perhaps patterns will emerge automatically and theory will become unnecessary.

Week 1 rejects this conclusion.

Computers can identify statistical structure. They cannot make archaeological questions disappear. Researchers still decide what to digitize, what variables represent, which categories to use, what counts as similarity, which outcomes matter, and how a computational pattern relates to human behavior.

The claim that “data replace theory” therefore hides theoretical decisions rather than eliminating them.

13. The Proxy Gap

The proxy gap is one of the most important concepts in the second half of the lecture.

Archaeological data are usually not digital-born. The past consisted of physical people, things, actions, deposits, buildings, landscapes, and relationships. A computational system does not encounter those realities directly.

Instead, it receives representations:

  • pixel values from imagery;
  • elemental or isotopic measurements;
  • 3D coordinates;
  • artifact measurements;
  • database categories;
  • text descriptions;
  • labels assigned by researchers;
  • derived variables.

Orengo and colleagues emphasize that machine-learning systems learn mathematical relationships among these representations. They do not learn an archaeological object “itself.”

A simplified chain is:

past phenomenon → surviving material trace → archaeological recognition → recording/measurement → digital representation → model input → model output → archaeological interpretation

Every transformation can introduce selection, uncertainty, or bias.

A model can be mathematically excellent at learning the proxy it was given while still answering the archaeological question poorly.

14. Inside the Black Box

Some machine-learning systems, especially deep-learning models, can be difficult to interpret internally. High predictive accuracy can therefore create false confidence.

Imagine a classifier that identifies pottery types with 95% accuracy. That sounds impressive. But what did it learn?

Perhaps it learned vessel shape and decoration. Or perhaps photographs of one type were usually taken on a particular background, under different lighting, or with a distinctive scale card.

The model may achieve high validation scores using a cue that is statistically useful but archaeologically meaningless.

Important questions therefore include:

  • What exactly was the model trained to predict?
  • How were labels created?
  • What is represented in the training data?
  • Are training and test data genuinely independent?
  • Could the model exploit accidental correlations?
  • Does performance transfer to another site, collection, or recording system?
  • Can archaeologists understand enough of the model to evaluate its archaeological relevance?

The problem is not “computers versus archaeology.” It is whether computational success corresponds to archaeological meaning.

15. Erasing the Specialist?

Automation can make complex analysis accessible to people who do not understand the full disciplinary process behind it. This can be useful, but it creates a danger: archaeology can be reduced to data engineering.

Domain expertise matters because specialists know:

  • how deposits form;
  • how typologies were constructed;
  • where classifications are uncertain;
  • how samples were collected;
  • which measurements are proxies;
  • how preservation affects observations;
  • which historical assumptions shaped a dataset;
  • what alternative explanations are archaeologically plausible.

A model can automate part of an analysis. It cannot make the interpretive history of the data irrelevant.

The goal is not to protect expertise from technology. It is to integrate computational power with archaeological accountability.

16. Toxic Training Data and the Hallucinated Past

Generative AI introduces a related problem.

AI systems learn from large bodies of existing text and imagery. Those materials contain outdated scholarship, stereotypes, popular misconceptions, unequal representation, and repeated visual conventions.

Magnani and Clindaniel’s study of AI-generated Neanderthal representations is useful here. They found substantial distance between generated content and current archaeological scholarship, including dated knowledge and cultural anachronisms. Adding “expert” language to a prompt does not guarantee that a model has consulted or reproduced current expert knowledge.

This creates two different problems.

Inherited bias

A model can reproduce old assumptions because those assumptions are common in its training materials.

Hallucination

A generative model can produce fluent, plausible material that is unsupported or false. Fluency is not evidence.

For archaeology, this is especially dangerous because plausible stories can easily fill gaps in an already fragmentary record.

Plausibility is not the same as evidential support.

17. Methods Driving Questions

Powerful tools can reverse the normal logic of research.

Instead of:

archaeological question → appropriate method

research can become:

available method → find something it can analyze

Machine learning often prefers structured, digitized, sufficiently large datasets. This can encourage researchers to ask questions determined by what is computationally convenient rather than what is archaeologically important.

The lecture therefore insists that deductive inquiry must steer the machine. This does not mean every project must use formal hypothesis testing. It means research questions and archaeological reasoning should determine method choice, not the prestige or novelty of technology.

Before using a computational method, ask:

  1. What archaeological question am I trying to answer?
  2. What observable consequences would different interpretations produce?
  3. What data could bear on those consequences?
  4. What does the method actually measure or classify?
  5. What assumptions connect its output to my archaeological claim?

18. AI-Aware Archaeological Learning

The lecture does not advocate banning AI. It argues for an AI-aware approach.

The course principle is augmentation, not automation.

AI can be useful for generating questions, exposing assumptions, suggesting alternative explanations, clarifying difficult concepts, or helping a student test an argument. But it should not replace the primary intellectual work of reading sources, evaluating evidence, making judgments, and constructing an argument.

This is closely connected to archaeological theory. If interpretation depends on understanding how claims are connected to evidence, then accepting a machine-generated answer without reconstructing that connection defeats the purpose of learning interpretation.

Critical deconstruction

Treat an AI response as an object for analysis:

  • What is its main claim?
  • What evidence does it provide?
  • Are the cited sources real and relevant?
  • What assumptions connect the evidence to the claim?
  • What alternatives are omitted?
  • Does the answer express uncertainty appropriately?
  • Can the claims be verified in original scholarly sources?

AI is not itself historical or archaeological evidence. Its output must be checked against the scholarly and material sources relevant to the question.

19. Co-Creation with Friction

The lecture’s phrase co-creation with friction captures a productive relationship with technology.

A useful tool should not remove every difficulty. Some intellectual difficulty is where learning occurs.

If AI writes the interpretation, chooses the evidence, supplies the argument, and evaluates the alternatives, the student may receive a polished product while learning very little about archaeological reasoning.

A better workflow is:

  1. You read first.
  2. You identify the archaeological problem.
  3. You form a preliminary interpretation.
  4. Use AI to challenge it: ask for assumptions, counterarguments, or alternative explanations.
  5. Return to the readings and evidence.
  6. Decide what survives criticism.
  7. Write the final argument in your own reasoning.

Human agency remains central because archaeological interpretation requires judgment about relevance, context, uncertainty, and meaning.

20. The Potential Past

The lecture ends its AI discussion with a constructive possibility.

AI and computational simulation need not function as machines that announce “the truth” about the past. They may be more useful for exploring structured possibilities.

The idea of the potential past shifts the question from:

“Can a computer reconstruct exactly what happened?”

to:

“Given specified evidence, assumptions, and constraints, what ranges of past processes are plausible, and what consequences would they produce?”

This is compatible with the broader Week 1 argument.

Theory provides premises and constraints. Models explore consequences. Archaeological evidence then helps evaluate whether those consequences resemble observed patterns.

The result is not factual certainty produced by an algorithm. It is a transparent exploration of possibilities.

21. Epistemic Stewardship

Archaeologists inherit records created by past people and by generations of researchers. They also create records that future researchers will use.

Epistemic stewardship means taking responsibility for how archaeological knowledge is produced, represented, preserved, and transmitted.

That includes:

  • documenting how data were collected;
  • preserving context and provenance;
  • recording uncertainty;
  • making classifications explicit;
  • distinguishing observation from inference;
  • protecting specialist knowledge from algorithmic flattening;
  • maintaining reproducible and transparent workflows where possible;
  • recognizing ethical responsibilities to communities connected to the past;
  • resisting the temptation to present computational outputs as self-interpreting facts.

This is why theory is not an optional philosophical decoration. Theory makes the assumptions of archaeological knowledge available for examination.

22. The Human Element

The final slide returns to people.

Archaeology is about human lives, and archaeological knowledge is produced by human communities of researchers, students, specialists, descendant communities, heritage professionals, and others.

AI may accelerate analysis and expand what can be explored, but empathy, contextual judgment, ethical responsibility, and care cannot simply be reduced to model outputs.

The Week 1 argument therefore comes full circle:

material remains are silent → humans construct interpretations → interpretations require theory → evidence constrains those interpretations → uncertainty must remain visible → computational tools can assist but do not remove human responsibility.

23. Guided Practice

Exercise A: From observation to explanation

You excavate a grave containing an adult skeleton, a bronze object, two ceramic vessels, and animal bone.

Separate the following:

  1. What could be directly observed?
  2. What would need to be recorded as data?
  3. What research questions could turn those data into evidence?
  4. What interpretations might be proposed?
  5. What assumptions would connect grave goods to social identity?
  6. What alternative explanations should be considered?
  7. What additional evidence would help discriminate among them?

The point is not to find one correct answer. The point is to expose the inferential steps.

Exercise B: The record bites back

Choose an archaeological claim from one of the readings. Ask:

  • What observations support it?
  • What result would make the claim less plausible?
  • Could the claim survive any possible evidence?

If no imaginable evidence could count against a claim, consider how archaeologists could evaluate it.

Exercise C: Audit an AI answer

Ask a generative AI a simple archaeological question. Do not assume the answer is correct.

Create three columns:

AI claim Verification source Result
Claim 1 Original reading/article supported / unsupported / overstated
Claim 2 Original reading/article supported / unsupported / overstated
Claim 3 Original reading/article supported / unsupported / overstated

Then identify one theoretical assumption and one omitted alternative.

24. Reading Guide

Catherine J. Frieman, “Attending to Unproof”

Focus on:

  • the irreparable fragmentation of archaeological data;
  • underdetermination;
  • the danger of unwarranted certainty;
  • unproof as gaps, ambiguity, and unknowns;
  • why uncertainty can open interpretive possibilities rather than merely represent failure.

Ask: What changes when archaeologists treat uncertainty as something to analyze rather than hide?

Colin Renfrew and Paul Bahn, Introduction

Focus on:

  • archaeology as the study of the human past through material remains;
  • archaeology as both science and humanity;
  • the difference between material remains and statements in written sources;
  • the need to construct rather than simply discover a picture of the past;
  • the relationship among evidence, hypothesis, model, method, and interpretation.

Ask: Why does the material nature of archaeological evidence make interpretation unavoidable?

Renfrew and Bahn, Chapter 1, “The Searchers”

Focus on:

  • the history of archaeology as a history of questions and ideas, not merely discoveries;
  • the interaction among theory, research methods, and field discoveries;
  • how changing social and scientific contexts alter archaeological interpretation.

Ask: If the material record was already present, what changed when archaeology developed new questions and methods?

Alison Wylie, Thinking from Things

Focus on:

  • the relationship between philosophy and archaeological practice;
  • theory-laden evidence;
  • hypothesis testing and the limits of simple positivism;
  • archaeological evidence that challenges expectations;
  • how stronger inference can emerge from the interaction of different evidential resources.

Ask: How can archaeological evidence be constructed through theory and still have the power to constrain theory?

25. Core Concepts to Know

Concept Short definition
Archaeological theory Concepts and assumptions used to connect material evidence with claims about the past
Theory-ladenness Observation and recording are shaped by prior concepts, questions, and expectations
Evidence Data made relevant to a particular claim or question
Inference Reasoning that connects evidence to a conclusion
Underdetermination Evidence may support more than one serious interpretation
Unproof Gaps, ambiguities, unknowns, and potentially unknowable aspects of the record
Empirical constraint The capacity of material evidence to make some interpretations difficult to sustain
Constructive bootstrapping Strengthening inference through mutually constraining lines of evidence
Proxy An indirect measurable representation used to investigate a phenomenon
Proxy gap The distance between past material reality and the measurements/representations used computationally
Black box A system whose internal basis for an output is difficult to inspect or interpret
Epistemic stewardship Responsibility for how knowledge and its evidential foundations are created and transmitted
Potential past A structured range of plausible past conditions or processes rather than a claim of certain reconstruction

  • Can I describe an appropriate role for AI in archaeological learning?

27. Key Takeaways

  1. The archaeological record does not interpret itself. Material traces become evidence through questions, concepts, and inferential relationships.

  2. There is no genuinely theory-free archaeology. Even apparently simple acts of observation, classification, and recording contain assumptions.

  3. Theory-ladenness does not make evidence powerless. Material traces can contradict expectations and force interpretations to change.

  4. Archaeological knowledge is often underdetermined. More than one interpretation may fit surviving evidence, and responsible scholarship keeps uncertainty visible.

  5. Strong arguments expose their inferential bridges. Ask what assumptions connect observations to claims and what alternatives remain possible.

  6. Multiple lines of evidence can create stronger constraints. Convergence is especially valuable when different methods do not all depend on the same assumptions.

  7. Big data do not eliminate theory. Computational methods still depend on choices about questions, variables, categories, proxies, and interpretation.

  8. Machine learning learns representations, not the past itself. Archaeological data are indirect proxies shaped by recording and classification practices.

  9. Generative AI produces plausible outputs, not archaeological authority. Claims must be checked against original scholarly and archaeological sources.

  10. Human judgment remains central. AI can augment archaeological reasoning, but responsibility for evidence, interpretation, uncertainty, ethics, and meaning remains with people.

One-Sentence Summary

Archaeological interpretation is the disciplined, theoretically informed process of turning incomplete material traces into evidence-based claims while allowing the material record, alternative explanations, and acknowledged uncertainty to constrain what we can responsibly say about the past.

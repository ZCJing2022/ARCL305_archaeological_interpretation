Week 2 — Archaeological Evidence

Week 2 Overview

Week 1 established that archaeological remains do not interpret themselves. Week 2 asks a more concrete question:

What exactly is archaeological evidence, how did it become what survives today, and how do archaeologists turn physical remains into evidence about past human life?

The lecture begins with Martin Carver’s deliberately plain term “the stuff”: broken pottery, stone tools, animal bones, seeds, walls, pits, soils, collapsed buildings, refuse, and countless other physical traces. Archaeology starts with these material remains, but the remains encountered today are not intact copies of past life. They are the products of human action, abandonment, decay, burial, disturbance, preservation, excavation, classification, and interpretation.

past life ≠ archaeological record

The archaeological record is what survives after a long history of transformations.

Week 2 therefore moves through three connected problems:

  1. What survives? — kinds and scales of archaeological evidence.
  2. How did it survive? — context, formation processes, taphonomy, preservation, stratigraphy, and time averaging.
  3. How does it become evidence? — conceptual scaffolding that makes physical traces relevant to claims about the past.

The required readings approach these problems from complementary directions. Carver emphasizes physical “stuff” and its continuous itinerary of decay. Renfrew and Bahn provide the vocabulary of artifacts, ecofacts, features, context, association, and formation processes. Lucas asks what kind of entity the archaeological record is and stresses formation, temporality, assemblages, and archaeological practice. Taylor and May show that stratigraphic recording is itself part of knowledge production. Wylie explains why evidence is constructed through conceptual and technical scaffolding yet can still surprise us and “bite back.”

Learning Objectives

By the end of Week 2, you should be able to explain why the archaeological record is a transformed material residue rather than a direct copy of the past; distinguish spatial and temporal scales; define context as matrix, provenience, and association; distinguish primary and secondary context and systemic and archaeological context; identify the major scales and categories of evidence; explain cultural and natural formation processes and taphonomy; critique the “Pompeii premise”; explain stratigraphy, superposition, time averaging, palimpsests, and Harris matrices; and explain how conceptual scaffolding allows theory-laden evidence to constrain interpretation.

1. Archaeology Begins with “The Stuff”

Carver begins from a simple physical fact: archaeology encounters material things in states of survival, decay, fragmentation, and displacement.

A standing temple may become a heap of stone. A timber building may collapse, rot, and survive only as discolorations in soil. A temporary shelter may leave little more than a patch of ash. A body may survive as bone, preserved flesh, a stain, or a void depending on burial conditions.

This is the stuff of archaeology.

The phrase prevents us from beginning with an already reconstructed past. Archaeologists do not excavate “a household economy,” “a political hierarchy,” or “a ritual system.” They excavate material traces from which such interpretations may eventually be developed.

Carver’s central question is:

Why do we have what we have?

A useful model is:

human action → material consequences → deposition → transformation → survival → discovery → recording → evidence → interpretation

Every arrow matters.

2. Evidence Exists at Multiple Scales

The lecture stresses that evidence operates simultaneously at many spatial and temporal scales.

Spatial scales

microscopic attribute → artifact/ecofact → feature → activity area → assemblage → component → site → region → landscape

A microscopic residue may reveal what a vessel contained. Its position beside a hearth may help define an activity area. Repeated activity areas may reveal household organization. Household patterns across a settlement may reveal neighborhood structure. Settlement distributions across a valley may illuminate regional organization.

Different questions require different scales.

Temporal scales

A stone flake may have been struck in less than a second. A hearth may represent hours or days. A house may accumulate deposits over decades. A tell may contain thousands of years.

Lucas emphasizes time averaging: materials deposited at different moments can become combined into one archaeological unit or assemblage. Archaeologists must ask whether a pattern represents one event, repeated behavior, or long-term accumulation.

Scale is not just size. Scale determines what kind of historical claim the evidence can support.

3. The Primacy of Context

Renfrew and Bahn define archaeological context through three dimensions:

Element Meaning Question
Matrix Physical material surrounding a find What was it embedded in?
Provenience Precise horizontal and vertical location Exactly where was it?
Association Relationship with other finds/features What was found with it?

A ceramic vessel by itself may reveal clay, manufacture, shape, decoration, and date. If it is found inside a grave beside a skeleton, with food remains, beneath a sealed layer, and with ornaments, its evidential possibilities expand because it is embedded in a relational network.

Looting therefore destroys more than objects’ locations. It destroys relationships that often cannot be reconstructed.

Archaeologists excavate relationships, not simply things.

4. Primary and Secondary Context

An object is in primary context when it remains where it was originally deposited or discarded. It is in secondary context when later processes have moved it.

River transport, ploughing, animal burrowing, later construction, erosion, looting, and redeposition can all create secondary contexts.

Secondary context does not make an object useless. It changes the questions that can safely be asked. A transported stone tool may still reveal technology or raw material, but its present location may not identify where it was used.

5. Systemic Context and Archaeological Context

Michael Schiffer’s distinction is central.

Systemic context refers to material while participating in a living behavioral system:

acquisition → manufacture → use → maintenance → reuse/recycling → discard

Objects may also be stored, exchanged, curated, lost, scavenged, cached, or reclaimed.

Archaeological context refers to material after deposition and subsequent transformations, as encountered archaeologically.

Archaeologists observe material relationships in the present but want to understand dynamic activities in the past. Formation theory and middle-range reasoning help bridge that gap.

The archaeological record is not the living system after somebody pressed “pause.”

6. Artifact Biography

An artifact may pass through many stages: raw-material selection, manufacture, use, repair, recycling, breakage, discard, post-depositional movement, and recovery.

A broken projectile point in a settlement does not automatically mean projectile use occurred where it was excavated. It could represent manufacture waste, repair, reuse, collection from elsewhere, or redeposited refuse.

Renfrew and Bahn similarly emphasize acquisition, manufacture, use, and discard. Remains can enter the record at any stage.

Which stage of an object’s life is represented by this find?

7. Intellectual Context

Archaeological context is also intellectual: research questions, theoretical concepts, categories, and disciplinary traditions shape what archaeologists recognize and record.

Culture-history, processualism, postprocessual approaches, and material-engagement approaches can direct attention toward different properties and relationships.

Legacy datasets therefore require historical understanding. Earlier excavations may have been recorded according to questions and categories that no longer dominate archaeology. Later researchers need to reconstruct those assumptions before reusing the evidence.

8. A Taxonomy of Physical Evidence

Attributes

Attributes are observable or measurable properties: shape, dimensions, color, raw material, decoration, manufacturing traces, wear, chemistry, and residues.

Attributes matter because of research questions. Measurement alone does not create archaeological significance.

Artifacts

Artifacts are portable objects made, modified, or used by humans. They may provide evidence about technology, craft, exchange, consumption, and identity, but must be interpreted through attributes, biographies, and contexts.

Ecofacts

Ecofacts are biological or environmental remains of archaeological significance: animal bones, shells, seeds, charcoal, pollen, phytoliths, soils, and sediments.

They may reveal diet, agriculture, animal management, climate, vegetation, fuel use, and environmental modification.

Features

Features are non-portable archaeological traces or modifications: hearths, postholes, pits, ditches, walls, floors, kilns, and graves.

A feature may contain several contexts—for example, a pit cut plus multiple fills and later disturbances.

9. Activity Areas

An activity area is a spatial concentration of traces associated with a task or set of tasks: knapping, butchery, cooking, bead production, pottery firing, and so on.

Activity areas emerge from distributions and associations rather than one diagnostic object. Ethnoarchaeology can help explain patterning, but cleaning, trampling, later occupation, animals, and erosion may alter it.

10. Assemblages

An assemblage is a related set of archaeological materials, but “related” requires clarification.

Lucas distinguishes a depositional assemblage, consisting of things physically recovered together, from a typological assemblage, an analytical grouping created because objects share selected characteristics.

Objects excavated together may have different histories; objects grouped typologically may never have existed together.

Whenever you see “assemblage,” ask: related in what sense?

11. Components and Occupation Phases

A component refers to material associated with a particular occupation or temporal/cultural phase of a site.

Multi-component sites may contain evidence from many occupations. Deep tell sequences result from repeated construction, demolition, rebuilding, refuse accumulation, and abandonment.

A component is not simply “a layer.” It is an analytical attempt to identify a coherent occupation or phase from stratigraphic and material relationships.

12. Sites, Regions, and Landscapes

A site is a place where significant traces of human activity are identified. It can range from an ephemeral camp to an urban settlement occupied for millennia.

The lecture distinguishes residential sites from non-residential sites used for specialized extraction, production, ritual, burial, transport, or defense. These categories can overlap.

Regional archaeology examines distributions across larger areas. Landscape archaeology asks how people inhabited, modified, organized, and understood environments.

Archaeological evidence is relational at every scale.

13. The Dynamic Itinerary of Decay

Carver argues that sites are not static snapshots waiting underground. They follow a dynamic itinerary of decay.

A building may be occupied, repaired, abandoned piecemeal, robbed, collapsed, buried, cut by later pits, disturbed by roots, mixed by animals, ploughed, excavated, conserved, and exposed again.

Formation can begin during occupation and continues after burial.

A better question than simply “What happened here?” is:

What happened here, what traces did those activities produce, and what happened to those traces afterward?

14. Site Formation Processes

Site formation processes create, modify, preserve, move, or destroy archaeological remains.

Cultural formation processes include manufacture, use, maintenance, recycling, discard, cleaning, storage, construction, demolition, burial, rebuilding, looting, ploughing, and excavation.

Natural formation processes include weathering, erosion, sedimentation, flooding, chemical alteration, microbial decay, roots, burrowing, wind, and freeze-thaw cycles.

Actual deposits usually result from interactions among several processes.

The archaeological record is produced, not merely preserved.

15. Taphonomy: Hunters or Hunted?

Taphonomy studies the transitions affecting organic remains from death to discovery. It asks who or what accumulated remains, whether carcasses were hunted or scavenged, whether bones moved, and whether marks came from tools, teeth, trampling, roots, or weathering.

The lecture’s “Hunters or Hunted?” example shows why this matters. Associations among early hominin remains, animal bones, and stone tools once encouraged simple hunting narratives. Detailed analysis of carnivore behavior and bone modification showed that some assemblages had very different formation histories.

Renfrew and Bahn discuss Swartkrans, where damage to a juvenile hominin cranium could be related to a leopard’s lower canines. The larger lesson is methodological:

Association is not automatically causation. Before reconstructing behavior, determine how the association formed.

16. Anomalies of Preservation

Preservation is selective. Stone and fired ceramics usually survive more readily than wood, textiles, basketry, food, and leather. But unusual environments can reverse the normal pattern.

Waterlogging and anaerobic deposits can slow microbial decay; aridity can desiccate organics; freezing can preserve tissues; charring can preserve plants. Soil chemistry may preserve one material while destroying another.

Carver’s examples make this vivid: bone may survive in alkaline conditions but disappear in acidic sand; bogs may preserve flesh while dissolving mineral bone.

Exceptional deposits expose the missing organic majority of ordinary sites.

What survives most often is not necessarily what mattered most in past life.

17. Deconstructing the “Pompeii Premise”

Pompeii is compelling because rapid catastrophic burial produced unusual preservation. This can encourage a misleading model of sites as frozen moments.

Most sites are nothing like Pompeii. People clean, remove valuables, repair structures, recycle materials, move refuse, abandon spaces gradually, and return later. Natural and cultural processes continue after abandonment.

Even Pompeii requires formation analysis: inhabitants fled with possessions, and earlier cleaning and remodeling had already shaped what was present.

An excavated floor is not automatically a photograph of everyday life.

18. Experimental Formation Research

One way to study formation is experimental archaeology.

Renfrew and Bahn discuss the Overton Down earthwork experiment, in which a modern bank and ditch and buried materials were monitored through time. Carver describes Sutton Hoo experiments testing decomposition and mound erosion. Some expected processes failed to produce the excavated patterns, forcing reinterpretation.

This is middle-range research:

observable present processes ↔︎ material consequences ↔︎ archaeological patterns

Experiments do not recreate the past exactly. They test whether a proposed process can plausibly produce an observed effect.

19. Stratigraphy: Putting Deposits into Order

Stratigraphy studies sequences and relationships among deposits, surfaces, cuts, structures, and other units.

The principle of superposition states that, in an undisturbed sequence, lower deposits were laid down before deposits above them.

Real stratigraphy is rarely a neat layer cake. People dig pits into older layers, walls cut deposits, floors are resurfaced, graves cut graves, erosion removes material, and later fills contain older artifacts.

Archaeologists therefore record relationships: above/below, cuts/is cut by, fills/is filled by, seals/is sealed by, and contemporaneity where justified.

Stratigraphy provides relative chronology: order, not automatically calendar dates.

20. The Palimpsest

A palimpsest is a record in which traces from multiple events or periods overlap.

A surface scatter may combine many occupations. A ploughed soil may contain vertically moved artifacts. A room fill may combine rubbish from several sources. A landscape may preserve monuments from many periods visible together.

A palimpsest is not simply bad evidence. It may be excellent for long-term patterns while poor for reconstructing a single event.

21. Time Averaging

Time averaging occurs when materials produced at different times become combined in one analytical unit.

If a deposit accumulated over 200 years, treating its pottery, bones, seeds, and tools as one household at one moment would mislead. Yet the same deposit may be valuable for long-term consumption or environmental patterns.

The temporal resolution of the claim should not exceed the temporal resolution of the evidence.

22. Harris Matrices: Relationships Rather Than Depth

A Harris matrix is a diagram of stratigraphic relationships among contexts. It models relative sequence rather than merely physical depth.

A simple sequence might be:

natural subsoil → pit cut → pit fill → occupation surface → later wall → demolition deposit

Taylor and May emphasize that the matrix is part of archaeological knowledge creation. Archaeologists define units, record relationships, construct and revise matrices, group contexts, establish phases, integrate finds and dates, and build site narratives.

The distinction between objective field recording and later subjective interpretation is therefore too simple. Interpretation already occurs “at the trowel’s edge.”

23. From Contexts to Phases

Archaeologists often move through:

stratigraphic units → recorded relationships → matrix → groups → phases → periods → site narrative

Separate postholes may be grouped into a building. Building episodes may form a settlement phase. Multiple phases may become a broader period.

Each step adds interpretation. Taylor and May stress documenting these transformations so later researchers can understand how observations became published narratives.

24. Stratigraphic Data Have an Afterlife

Excavation is destructive. Once a context is removed, future researchers depend on archives: context records, plans, sections, photographs, matrices, databases, samples, phasing, specialist analyses, and spatial data.

Taylor and May argue that stratigraphic data should be FAIR: Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable.

This is epistemological, not merely technical. If relationships underlying an interpretation cannot be reconstructed, later scholars cannot effectively test or revise it.

Good archaeological recording preserves the possibility of future disagreement.

25. Conceptual Scaffolding

Wylie’s key contribution is scaffolding.

Physical traces do not become evidence by themselves. Archaeologists use background knowledge, methods, concepts, and practices to make observations relevant to claims.

Scaffolding can include stratigraphy, association, formation theory, taphonomy, experiments, dating, typologies, chemical sourcing, analogy, comparative collections, statistics, recording conventions, and specialist expertise.

For example, cut marks on bone become evidence of butchery only through knowledge of tool marks, carnivore damage, carcass processing, weathering, and genuine association.

The inference depends on scaffolding.

26. Scaffolding Can Hide Evidence

Scaffolding is necessary but can become restrictive.

Typologies, recording systems, chronologies, and field conventions were often designed for particular questions. Once standardized, they can persist after those questions are forgotten. A classification may make some patterns visible and others difficult to recognize.

The goal is not to eliminate scaffolding. It is to make it visible, revisable, and accountable.

27. How Evidence “Bites Back”

If evidence is theory-laden, why is archaeology not circular?

Wylie’s answer is that material evidence can turn out differently than expected. New observations may contradict chronology, reveal non-contemporaneity, expose unexpected formation histories, undermine a behavioral interpretation, or force a typology to change.

Conceptually mediated evidence can still resist the expectations that helped produce it.

This is especially powerful when partly independent lines of evidence converge or new methods reveal properties earlier researchers could not observe.

28. Reusing Old Evidence

Old collections and archives are not necessarily exhausted. New questions and methods can make overlooked properties evidentially relevant.

Ceramics may be reexamined with petrography, residue analysis, XRF, new typologies, network analysis, or improved chronology. Legacy excavation records may reveal new relationships when reconstructed digitally.

But reuse requires understanding original scaffolding: what was collected, discarded, categorized, sampled, and recorded—and what was never recorded.

30. Worked Example: A Hearth and Scatter

Imagine excavating a circular patch of charcoal surrounded by stone flakes and animal bone.

A quick interpretation might be: “People sat around a hearth making tools and eating meat.”

Apply Week 2 reasoning.

Describe: charcoal-rich deposit, burned stones, flakes, bone, spatial clustering.

Establish context: Are the materials in the same matrix? What are their precise positions? Is the hearth cut by later features?

Examine formation: Are bones burned? Could water, trampling, animals, or cleaning have moved materials?

Establish temporal resolution: One event, repeated hearth use, or decades of accumulation?

Apply middle-range scaffolding: hearth experiments, lithic refitting, taphonomy, ethnoarchaeological drop/toss models.

Compare alternatives: one domestic episode; repeated occupations; secondary refuse around an old hearth; mixed post-abandonment deposits.

The “obvious” interpretation becomes an argument that must earn support.

31. Guided Practice

Exercise A: Context audit

Choose an artifact from the lecture. Record what can be learned from the object alone, what requires matrix, what requires provenience, what requires association, and what becomes unknowable if it was looted.

Exercise B: Formation biography

Take an ordinary object and trace:

manufacture → use → maintenance → reuse → discard/loss → burial → natural transformation → later disturbance → excavation

Which stages leave traces? Which disappear?

Exercise C: Pompeii premise

You find a room containing five pots, a grinding stone, animal bones, and ash. Produce one interpretation assuming a frozen activity moment and another assuming gradual accumulation, cleaning, reuse, and abandonment. What evidence would distinguish them?

Exercise D: Harris matrix

Imagine natural soil; a pit cut into it; pit fill; a floor over the filled pit; a later posthole cutting the floor; and posthole fill. Put the units into relative order. Which relationships are observations, and which later groupings are interpretations?

32. Reading Guide

Alison Wylie, “How Archaeological Evidence Bites Back”

Focus on data and evidence as constructed rather than simply given; background knowledge and conceptual scaffolding; established categories and conventions; empirical resistance; and the possibility of putting old evidence to new uses.

Question: How can evidence be constructed through archaeological practice and still constrain archaeological claims?

Martin Carver, Chapter 1, “The Stuff”

Focus on material survival, decay, dispersal, preservation, “why do we have what we have?”, systemic and archaeological context, formation processes, ethnoarchaeology, experiment, and microscopic approaches.

Question: What processes transformed living places into the traces archaeologists encounter?

Gavin Lucas, Understanding the Archaeological Record

Focus on competing meanings of “archaeological record”; remains versus archaeologist-produced archive; formation theory; systemic and archaeological context; time averaging; assemblages; and archaeological intervention.

Question: Is the archaeological record something surviving from the past, something archaeologists construct, or both?

Renfrew and Bahn, Chapter 2, “What Is Left?”

Focus on artifacts, ecofacts, features, sites, and regions; matrix, provenience, and association; primary/secondary context; cultural/natural formation; artifact life cycles; preservation; experiments; and superposition.

Question: Why must archaeologists reconstruct the history of a find before using it as evidence for behavior?

Taylor and May, “Resurrecting, Reinterpreting and Reusing Stratigraphy”

Focus on stratigraphic units and Harris matrices; recording versus interpretation; grouping and phasing; digital archives; FAIR data; and reuse.

Question: What must be preserved so future researchers can reconstruct and challenge today’s interpretations?

35. Key Takeaways

  1. Archaeology begins with material “stuff,” not ready-made history.
  2. The archaeological record is transformed residue, not a direct copy of past life.
  3. Context is relational. Matrix, provenience, and association turn isolated finds into stronger evidence.
  4. Evidence operates at multiple spatial and temporal scales.
  5. Systemic context and archaeological context are not the same. Formation theory connects them.
  6. Artifacts have biographies. Manufacture, use, reuse, discard, and post-depositional movement matter.
  7. Preservation is biased. Durable materials and exceptional environments distort what is visible.
  8. Most sites are not Pompeii. Deposits commonly combine repeated activities and later transformations.
  9. Taphonomy and formation analysis protect against false behavioral inference.
  10. Stratigraphy is relational chronology. Archaeologists reconstruct sequences from deposits, cuts, fills, and structures.
  11. Palimpsests and time averaging limit temporal resolution but can preserve long-term patterns.
  12. Harris matrices are models of evidential relationships and part of knowledge creation.
  13. Archaeological archives matter because excavation is destructive.
  14. Conceptual scaffolding is unavoidable. It makes evidence possible but can also hide alternatives.
  15. Theory-laden evidence can still bite back. Unexpected material relationships can force archaeologists to revise assumptions.

One-Sentence Summary

Archaeological evidence is not simply what survives from the past but what archaeologists can responsibly infer from material traces after reconstructing their context, formation, temporal relationships, preservation histories, and the conceptual scaffolding through which those traces become relevant to claims about human history.
